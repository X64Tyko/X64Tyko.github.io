Orc Incremental is an ambitious auto-battler with a deckbuilder-style army
construction mechanic. It has a genuinely satisfying core loop, and it is let
down by a lack of polish, a solved meta, and systems you cannot see. It feels
more like a prototype than a finished product.

I put fifty-three hours into it anyway, and I solved it on my second run.

Both of those are true, and the distance between them is the review. The army
building underneath is good enough to hold fifty-three hours of completionism.
The game makes it remarkably difficult to get those wheels turning.

## The core loop: building your horde

The gameplay is simple but effective. You start a run and are presented with a
random selection of three unit cards (expandable to four via Dark Ascension)
to add to your growing army. Do you add another Troll to your front line, or a
Witch Doctor to buff the units you already have? Once placed on the staging
area, your horde marches forward, automatically tearing through waves of
enemies.

Progression runs through five upgrade "trees" and a larger Dark Ascension
prestige tree. Ascensions are infinite. I stopped at fourteen, and only the
first five or so were distinguishable from each other in play, which is roughly
what it took to get past level 400. In later stages levels swell with thousands of enemies and
progress slows to a crawl, pushing you into very specific speed-clearing builds
and making mechanics like Warpath and Rage feel mandatory rather than strategic.

## The highs: synergistic mayhem

Totems are the heart of Orc Incremental's system design and its strongest
feature. These items allow units to trigger effects based on battlefield events,
enabling powerful cross-unit synergies. A "Creature" unit might gain rage when a
"Warrior" gets a kill, or your whole army might heal when 3 totems trigger
together.

Watching a fully-powered army tear through thousands of enemies is immensely
satisfying, and assembling the thing that does it is the actual game. When a
composition finally clicks, this is easily worth eight dollars.

## The lows: solved on run two

I unlocked every unit on my first playthrough. On my second I wanted to see what
Dragons did, picked up an **Iron Snake Totem** along the way, and that was that.
**Warriors** are close to a default pick already, so with **Trolls** in front the
whole composition assembled itself out of ordinary curiosity. I was not hunting
for a degenerate build. I was looking at dragons.

That combination trivializes all available content. It solves horde-clearing and
single-target boss damage at the same time, which invalidates most other units.
Juggernauts are the clearest casualty: their self-sacrifice mechanic works
directly against the army size that Warpath wants, so the unit actively fights
the strategy the game pushes you toward.

It hollows out whole systems, too. The upgrade trees and Dark Ascension both
put real emphasis on spells, and spells start with Fireball, which is your
click. This is an idler. I stopped clicking as soon as it was humanly possible
to stop.

Past that, in fifty-three hours I unlocked exactly one other spell: Well of
Despair, a little black hole. I cast it twice and never bought it again.

So a heavily invested pillar of the progression sits behind the one interaction
an idle game trains you out of. I could not tell you whether the spells are any
good. Nothing ever showed me what one did measured against what it cost, and the
button it lives on is the button I had already stopped pressing.

The economy makes it worse. You buy board slots and you buy the random units to
fill them, so every expansion is charged twice. Costs climb far faster than
income, which turns most of the midgame into a grind for stat boosts too small
to feel. Then difficulty spikes at level 500 and scales harder from there, as if
to discourage you from advancing at all. My best was somewhere around 540.
Getting from 510 to 540 took multiple resets and was abysmal.

## You cannot see anything

I tried to work out what the Iron Snake Totems were actually doing. The damage
numbers are there, which is the frustrating part. They are simply unreadable: by
late game the army is large enough and the snake procs often enough that the
numbers become a wash of digits nobody is totalling. So the only instrument I
actually had was "do the waves seem to clear faster with a second one, or should
that have been another one-cost unit?" That is not a measurement. That is a
feeling.

Data you cannot read is worse than no data, because it looks like the problem
has already been solved.

The same hole runs through the whole game. I finished all seven challenges, and
the bonuses were real and completely imperceptible in play, because nothing ever
shows you what changed. The progression trees are full of genuine nodes with
genuine values, and past a threshold the increments vanish into the noise. The
UI is clean to a fault: descriptions for upgrades and for mechanics like totem
stacking are vague or absent, and the trees are loosely themed but unorganized,
so finding a specific upgrade means hunting for it.

A solved meta is what happens next. When you cannot measure anything, you stop
experimenting and copy the one build that visibly works.

It is also why I stopped. My plan had been to unlock every node in the upgrade
tree. After four ascensions in a row that produced no new content and very
little progress, I put it down, because the only thing left to gain was a worse
opinion of a game I had enjoyed.

## Art and sound

Good indie spritework. Crisp, readable at thousands of units, and committed to
its look. Not amazing, and I had the audio off for most of my fifty-three hours,
which tells you what you need to know about the sound.

## Incomplete

Achievements are missing for the last two bosses. There is no endgame signal of
any kind, so the game does not end so much as stop being worth continuing.

## Lessons

Four things I am taking into my own designs.

**Show the player what your systems are doing.** This is the real one, and every
other complaint here is downstream of it. Bonuses that cannot be perceived are
bonuses that did not ship. If a player has to run controlled experiments with no
instruments to find out whether an item helped, they will stop running
experiments and copy a build instead, and the depth you designed goes unused.
The sharpest version of this is that a player can spend fifty-three hours
alongside an entire pillar of your progression and try exactly one thing in it,
not because the rest is bad but because nothing ever argued for it in terms they
could check.

**An active mechanic in an idler has to change shape as the game idles.**
Incentivising the click is the right instinct. Give the player something to do
with their hands and make it matter, especially early, when there is little else
happening. The mistake is leaving it a click forever. An idle game spends its
entire run teaching you that automation is the reward, and Orc Incremental then
parks a whole progression pillar behind the one button it trained you to stop
pressing. The intent should survive. The form should not. Let it become a
cooldown, a charge spent at the right moment, something that pays attention back
without demanding it continuously.

**Scaling has to be consistent and logical.** A difficulty wall dropped at level
500 does not create tension, it creates an exit. Arbitrary spikes feel punitive
and disrespect the player's time.

**An incremental still needs an ending.** Without an endgame signal there is no
closure, only the moment a player notices they have stopped.
